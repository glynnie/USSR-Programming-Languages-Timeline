# USSR Programming Languages Timeline

This README recreates the final two-page portrait handout as closely as practical in GitHub Markdown. PDF-only layout features such as exact card positioning, embedded page numbers, and clickable PDF annotations are approximated here with tables, headings, bullet points, and standard Markdown links.

## 1955 — Address Programming Language

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![Kateryna Yushchenko](images/yushchenko.jpg) | **Early Soviet high-level language** by Kateryna Yushchenko. Used for economic, military, aviation, and early space calculations. **Key points:**<br>- Created in Kyiv<br>- Advanced address/pointer ideas<br>- Applied to early technical planning<br><sub>Refs [1][2]</sub> |

## 1958–63 — ALMO

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![BESM-era Soviet computer](images/besm6_v2.jpg) | **Machine-oriented algorithmic system** used in compiler and systems work around major Soviet installations. **Key points:**<br>- Compiler-oriented design<br>- Close to machine structure<br>- Linked to BESM-era systems work<br><sub>Refs [2][3]</sub> |

## 1962–63 — LYaPAS

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![LYaPAS panel](images/panel_2.png) | **Tomsk language** for non-numeric and logic-style processing, later adapted to the Soviet GOST 10859 character set. **Key points:**<br>- Developed in Tomsk<br>- Non-numeric processing focus<br>- Adapted to GOST 10859<br><sub>Ref [4]</sub> |

## 1966–68 — Refal

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![Refal panel](images/panel_3.png) | **Symbolic pattern-matching language** used for translation, formula manipulation, and early AI-style work. **Key points:**<br>- Created by Valentin Turchin<br>- Strong symbolic manipulation<br>- Useful for translation and AI<br><sub>Refs [5][6]</sub> |

---

## 1978–79 — Rapira

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![Rapira system](images/img_4.jpg) | **Russian-syntax educational language** for Agat and Soviet PDP-11-compatible systems. **Key points:**<br>- Russian-style keywords<br>- Popular in education<br>- Ran on Agat and DVK-class machines<br><sub>Ref [7]</sub> |

## 1980s — Robik

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![Robik system](images/img_5.jpg) | **Child-focused descendant of Rapira** designed for primary-school teaching. **Key points:**<br>- Made for younger learners<br>- Simplified educational focus<br>- School-oriented rather than industrial<br><sub>Ref [7]</sub> |

## 1986 — Buran toolchain

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![Buran diagram](images/img_6.jpg) | **PROL2, DIPOL, and LAKS** supported onboard, ground, and modelling software during the Buran shuttle effort. **Key points:**<br>- Multiple specialist languages<br>- Aerospace mission software<br>- Set the stage for DRAKON<br><sub>Refs [8][9]</sub> |

## 1996 — DRAKON

| Image | Summary |
|---|---|
| ![DRAKON diagram](images/img_7.jpg) | **Visual language** completed after Buran and later reported in aerospace, oil, and nuclear-power procedures. **Key points:**<br>- Diagram-based notation<br>- Built for clarity and safety<br>- Later used beyond space projects<br><sub>Refs [9][10]</sub> |
